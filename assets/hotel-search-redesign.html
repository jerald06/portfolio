01 — Overview

The Brief

A B2B travel platform used vertical hotel cards in its search results — a common pattern borrowed from consumer travel apps. As the product scaled internationally, a critical UX flaw surfaced: the vertical card's fixed-width price column couldn't accommodate high-denomination currencies like INR, JPY, or IDR, where prices routinely exceed 6–7 digits.

Beyond the number overflow issue, the vertical card suffered from weak visual grouping, poor reading alignment, and a cramped information architecture that forced users to work hard to compare hotels.

"The card is the most important unit of the search experience — if the card fails, the entire search flow breaks down."

The redesign moved from a vertical stacked card to a horizontal layout with four clear visual zones: image, hotel identity, price breakdown, and call-to-action. This seemingly simple shift unlocked significant usability improvements.

02 — Problem Space

What Was Breaking

Analysis of the existing vertical card design revealed four interconnected failure points across information architecture, layout, and international usability.

Currency Overflow

High-denomination currencies (INR, JPY, IDR) caused 6–8 digit prices to overflow or wrap awkwardly in the narrow price column.

Weak Visual Hierarchy

Hotel name, price, and CTA competed for attention. No dominant focal point guided the eye through the card.

Poor Grouping

Related information (wholesale rate, resort fee, taxes, markup) was not visually grouped, making comparison slow and error-prone.

Limited Information Capacity

The vertical layout could not accommodate price breakdown details without extreme crowding, hiding key decision-making data.

03 — Design Decisions

The Horizontal Shift

The redesign was built around one core insight: a horizontal layout maps naturally to how users read search results — left to right, in a single pass. The vertical card forced users into a two-dimensional scanning pattern that slowed comparison.

Four Clear Zones

The new card divides into four semantically distinct, visually separated zones:

Solving the Currency Problem

The most critical functional fix was giving the price zone a generous, scalable width. In the vertical card, prices were constrained to a column matching the card width — meaning a 7-digit INR figure like ₹1,19,639.13 would overflow or require micro-font sizing.

The horizontal card's price zone grows laterally without affecting the image or hotel identity zones. Each price row has its own dedicated horizontal line, making high-denomination numbers completely legible at normal font sizes.

04 — What We Fixed & Why It Matters

Design Decisions That Made the Difference

Every change in the new card was a direct response to something that was causing real friction for users. Here's what was broken and how the redesign addressed it.

1

Users were reading left-to-right but the card made them scroll up and down

The vertical layout forced users to move their eyes in multiple directions just to read a single card. Switching to horizontal means hotel name, price, and the Select button all sit on one horizontal sweep — one glance, one decision.

2

Prices looked like a wall of numbers with no context

Wholesale rate, resort fee, taxes, and agency markup were stacked without any visual breathing room. Users couldn't quickly tell what they'd actually pay. The new layout groups these with clear spacing and labels so the breakdown reads like a mini receipt — familiar and scannable.

3

Important details were hidden unless you were already paying attention

The Total Price and the Select button are what most users need first. The full price breakdown is there for users who want to dig deeper. The new card surfaces the headline figures prominently in a dedicated zone on the right, while keeping the detailed breakdown visible but secondary.

4

The "Book" button was hard to find on a busy page

In the vertical card, the CTA sat at the bottom after a lot of content. On a results page with multiple cards, users had to hunt for it each time. In the redesign, the Select button anchors to the right edge of every card — same position, every time, easy to predict and click.

5

Comparing two hotels side by side was mentally exhausting

Because information was scattered across the card without clear structure, comparing two results meant remembering where each piece of data lived. The four-zone structure creates a consistent spatial map — users always know where the price is, where the amenities are, and where to act.

6

The card felt cluttered even when it wasn't showing that much

Tight spacing between elements made the card feel noisier than it actually was. Generous padding and clear internal separation in the new design don't just look better — they tell users "these things belong together" and "these things are separate" without any extra labels.

7

Prices in certain markets were simply breaking the layout

For users booking in INR, JPY, or IDR, the number of digits in a price regularly exceeded what the narrow vertical column could handle. The elastic width of the horizontal price zone means any currency, any denomination, renders cleanly — the layout doesn't break, the font doesn't shrink, and the number doesn't wrap mid-digit.

05 — Measurable Outcomes

Real-World Impact

After the redesigned card was pushed to production, user behaviour data was captured through Microsoft Clarity — tracking rage clicks and dead clicks as proxies for user frustration and confusion. The results showed a clear and meaningful improvement.

Microsoft Clarity — Before vs After

Rage clicks indicate user frustration (rapid repeated clicking when something isn't working). Dead clicks indicate users clicking on elements that don't respond — a sign of layout confusion or unclear interactive affordances. Both dropped significantly after the redesign went live.
